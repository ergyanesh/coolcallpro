Los Angeles Climate by the Numbers

NOAA's 1991-2020 Climate Normals record 1,420 heating degree days and 552 cooling degree days for Los Angeles — a heating-leaning climate where furnaces and heat pumps run more hours than central AC. In an average year, 2.5 days per year hit 90°F — short hot stretches rather than sustained heat.

Heating Operating Cost in Los Angeles: Heat Pump vs Gas Furnace

At Los Angeles's state-average rates (33.2¢/kWh electricity, $1.85/therm natural gas), the gas furnace wins on operating cost: $32.45/MMBtu for the heat pump vs $19.23/MMBtu for the gas furnace, a 41% margin. For a typical 1,500 sqft home in Los Angeles's climate (1,420 HDD), that maps to roughly $500/year for a heat pump vs $300/year for a gas furnace at current rates — actual usage varies with insulation, thermostat habits, and equipment efficiency. The math, transparently: 1 MMBtu = 293.07 kWh = 10 therms. Heat pump cost-per-MMBtu = (electricity rate × 293.07) ÷ COP; gas furnace cost-per-MMBtu = (gas rate × 10) ÷ AFUE. Modern heat pumps achieve a coefficient of performance (COP) of 2.5 to 4 across most U.S. climates per the U.S. Department of Energy; modern gas furnaces hit 95-98% AFUE.

Climate Profile (NOAA Station KLAX)

Los Angeles International Airport (KLAX) is the official NOAA reference station for the city. Per the NOAA NCEI U.S. Climate Normals 1991–2020 (station USW00023174), Los Angeles records an annual mean temperature of 63.6°F, an average annual maximum of 70.6°F against an annual minimum of 56.6°F, approximately 1,213.5 annual heating degree days against 720.4 cooling degree days, and an annual precipitation normal of 12.23 inches. The roughly 1.7:1 HDD-to-CDD ratio and narrow annual temperature range reflect LA’s coastal Mediterranean climate. Both heating and cooling loads are mild by national standards, but the sheer size of the housing stock means even modest per-unit HVAC investment translates to enormous aggregate demand.

Housing Stock & Heating-Fuel Distribution

The U.S. Census Bureau ACS 2023 5-year estimates (Tables B25040 and B25035 for Los Angeles city, California) report 1,419,663 occupied housing units — by far the largest housing stock of any project city — with a median year built of 1964. Heating-fuel distribution: 57.7% utility natural gas (818,490 units), 33.6% electricity (476,592 units), 6,550 homes using solar energy, and notably 90,123 homes using no fuel at all (6.3%). The 6.3% no-fuel share — over 90,000 homes that operate without any dedicated heating system — is the highest of any project city and reflects the coastal Mediterranean climate.

Distinctive: LADWP Municipal Utility & Scale

Los Angeles’s electric service is provided by the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power (LADWP), the largest municipally owned utility in the United States. LADWP administers its own residential rebate and electrification incentive programs. Contact LADWP directly for the current heat pump, smart thermostat, and central AC rebate amounts. LADWP’s municipal status means LA electric customers contract with the City.

Mild Climate & Electrification Opportunity (Zone 3B)

Per U.S. Department of Energy Energy Saver guidance, Zone 3B warm-dry climates like coastal Los Angeles produce moderate cooling loads with minimal humidity. The 12.23-inch annual precipitation and dry air mean latent loads are negligible. LA’s mild climate makes it ideal heat-pump territory: standard air-source heat pumps operate near peak efficiency year-round. California’s building electrification mandates and TECH Clean California program are accelerating gas-to-heat-pump conversion across the metro.

California HVAC Contractor Licensing (CSLB C-20)

California requires HVAC contractors to hold a C-20 Warm-Air Heating, Ventilating and Air-Conditioning (HVAC) License issued by the California Contractors State License Board (CSLB). Verify a specific contractor’s current CSLB C-20 license status before contracting.
